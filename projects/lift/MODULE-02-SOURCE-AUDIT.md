# Module 02 Private Source Audit

This is private operating context. Do not copy private provenance, generated
statistics, employer terminology, or identifying examples into LIFT.

## Source concepts reviewed

- manager-to-next-level context relay;
- naming contributors in forums where they are absent;
- accurate credit attribution;
- visible or stretch work assignment;
- formal documentation;
- manager advocacy capacity;
- manager pathways as a constraint on report visibility.

## Retain through rewrite

- advocacy is an observable management behavior, not a private feeling;
- managers should name specific contributions accurately;
- contribution needs context to travel across organizational layers;
- opportunity creation can be a form of sponsorship;
- managers should build direct evidence paths rather than making people depend
  forever on one relay;
- advocacy attention and organizational capital are finite;
- documentation and informal advocacy serve different purposes.

## Remove

- ranked behavioral correlations and dose-response tables;
- claims that one behavior universally outperforms another;
- a fixed limit of three actively sponsored reports;
- numerical penalties for advocating for more people;
- promotion probabilities and score effects;
- advice to concentrate support only on people closest to promotion;
- claims that ordinary support, defense, or documentation "does not count";
- the assumption that visibility is always desired or safe;
- employer-specific review, level, and promotion processes.

## Public rewrite thesis

Advocacy is the accurate movement of evidence, credit, context, and opportunity
through an organization.

Every team member deserves an advocacy floor:

- accurate attribution;
- clear expectations and feedback;
- fair consideration for opportunity;
- correction of material misrepresentation.

Additional sponsorship is capacity-constrained, but capacity does not justify
secret favoritism. Managers should make criteria visible, inspect distribution,
obtain consent, rotate access where appropriate, and build broader sponsorship
infrastructure.

## External comparison

Public literature supports adjacent claims:

- sponsorship uses influence and social capital to create opportunities;
- ambiguous group contribution can produce biased credit attribution;
- informal assignment systems can reproduce workforce inequality;
- effective mentorship and sponsorship require attention to access and
  inclusion.

No public source reviewed validates the private ranking, bandwidth threshold,
or promotion-effect numbers.
